Occoquan One Water decision tools

A fast, physics-based model that links pollution sources to drinking water quality, built into interactive tools that let utilities, regulators and communities explore choices together.

The problem

The Occoquan system is one of the first "One Water" systems in the United States, where treated wastewater and urban stormwater are part of the raw water supply for up to one million people in Northern Virginia. That is what makes pollutants hard to manage here. Reclaimed water is discharged to a stream just above the drinking water reservoir, while the watershed around it keeps developing, so road salt, PFAS and other contaminants arrive by several routes and on different timescales. Different agencies manage each route, and none of them had a shared way to test choices together.

Sodium is the long-running example. It has risen for four decades and now routinely exceeds the EPA health advisory for people on severely restricted sodium diets. The drinking water utility has looked at planning-level options such as a reverse osmosis upgrade that would likely cost more than $1 billion, before disposal and maintenance. Cutting pollution at its sources, such as road deicers and reclaimed water, may cost less. The tools below focus on sodium, and the approach is designed to extend to other pollutants such as PFAS.

The key idea: transit times

Conventional pollutant transport models resolve flow and chemistry in fine detail, which makes them heavy to calibrate and too slow to run in a live meeting. Transit-time theory takes a different route. It tracks how long water spends travelling through the system, the transit time distribution, along with how much of each source enters over time. With those two ingredients you can calculate the concentration of a dissolved pollutant at the intake at any moment.

One framework, many pollutants

The same structure handles conservative salts such as sodium and chloride and, with a transformation rate, pollutants that react along the way.

Very few parameters

The published version predicts sodium and chloride well with just two parameters, calibrated on 11 years of hydrologic and water quality data.

Fast enough for a live meeting

In the live meeting, the watershed simulation through 2100, transit times included, ran in about two minutes or less, so stakeholders could change an assumption and see the answer in the room.

The tools on this page used an early version of the method. The refined versions are published at two scales, both in Water Research.

The catchment: an urban stream

Applied to a deicer-impacted stream that drains to the reservoir. The model reproduces ten years of high-frequency salinity measurements and implies roughly one 20 kg bag of rock salt per person per year across the watershed's 20,000 residents. Stream water age emerges as a master variable: young snowmelt carries the highest chloride, while older groundwater sets an intermediate background level. Read the 2026 paper.

The system: the reservoir

Applied to the Occoquan Reservoir itself, linking pollution sources to drinking water quality in a One Water system. Read the 2025 paper, which is open access.

The vision

One model for water quantity and quality

The long-term idea is to couple this water-balance model with the transit-time model, so one tool simulates both how much water moves through the system and what it carries. How the models were used

  • Stakeholder committee meeting, November 2, 2023. Participants split into nine small groups of about five. Each group worked through Model 1 on a laptop with a trained facilitator, who asked what they chose and why. The sessions were recorded to study how stakeholders make decisions with a model.
  • NSF CIVIC focus groups, December 2024. More than 30 stakeholders from utilities, regulators, local governments and NGOs tested Model 2 across four focus groups, working through three exercises.
  • What came next. Participants asked for finer spatial detail, customizable scenarios and cost and performance data for PFAS controls. Their feedback shaped a product requirements document, a move from desktop toward a web app, and a full-award proposal submitted to NSF in March 2025. Drinking water and wastewater utilities and a county government committed in writing to use and support the platform.

Where this approach could go

The long-term goal is one model for the whole One Water system: the quantity of water and the quality it carries, across drinking water, wastewater and stormwater. These are applications the approach is built to support, not work already completed.

  • PFAS and other emerging contaminants. The planning grant scoped the same structure for PFAS scenarios and cost-performance data on mitigation.
  • Source control versus treatment. Putting upstream options next to a treatment upgrade, with costs, before capital is committed.
  • Drought, growth and conservation planning. Testing how sensitive supply and quality are to reclaimed water, demand and conservation choices.
  • Policy and permit changes. Exploring what a rule or guideline change would do across agencies before adopting it.
  • Other systems and classrooms. Any reservoir shared by several agencies, and teaching systems thinking in courses and community workshops.
